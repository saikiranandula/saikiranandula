# AI-Slop Blocklist & Self-Check (LinkedIn)

The failure mode that kills a thought-leadership profile is sounding like a generated LinkedIn post. A senior product leader or recruiter spots it in two lines and discounts everything after. It also costs reach: LinkedIn punishes templated content indirectly through low dwell, no saves, and no real discussion. So this file is a *distribution tool*, not just taste.

This is the enforceable checklist, distilled from **AISLOPOPEDIA** (the encyclopedia of AI tells, written by the model that produces them), extended with LinkedIn-native patterns and with the pattern set from `petergyang/no-ai-slop`. **Run every draft against the self-check before publishing: posts, carousels, and comments.**

Sai's edge is sounding like one specific product leader with specific evidence, not like the median LinkedIn post.

## How to use

These are *high-suspicion patterns*, not a banned-words list. One may be fine; a cluster means rewrite. The test: if a phrase is doing rhetorical work *instead of* carrying a fact, a number, a mechanism, or a real decision, cut it.

The source's own thesis: *"These patterns aren't bad because they're wrong. They're bad because they're overused and invisible. When everything sounds the same, nothing sounds like anything."*

## Openers (most damaging, they set the read)

- **Throat-clearers** (delay the point by a sentence): "Here's the thing:", "Here's what most people miss:", "Here's what nobody's talking about:", "Here's the uncomfortable truth:", "Let me be clear:", "I'll be honest:", "Can we talk about X for a second?", "Let's talk about X.", "We need to talk about X." → Just say the point. Open with the observed number, the contradiction, or the consequence.
- **False exclusivity:** "Nobody's talking about this.", "Most people won't tell you this.", "Everyone's sleeping on this.", "This flew under the radar.", "What they don't want you to know:", "The secret X doesn't want you to know:", "Most people get this completely wrong.", "99% of people do this backwards." → Real insight stands without the conspiracy framing.
- **Manufactured urgency:** "Stop what you're doing.", "Drop everything.", "Read this before X.", "Save this before it gets taken down.", "You're going to want to bookmark this.", "This changes everything.", "This is bigger than people realize.", "X just changed the game forever.", "PSA:".
- **Fake vulnerability and the manufactured story:** "I'm going to be honest with you.", "I wasn't going to post this, but…", "This is scary to share.", "Hot take incoming (don't hate me):", "Unpopular opinion:", "I might lose followers for this, but…", "I know I'll get hate for this, but…" (Reality: the "unpopular opinion" is always something 95% agree with.) Also the rejection or failure cold-open ("Last week I got rejected.", "I got fired in 2019.") and the too-neat anecdote that resolves into a tidy lesson. Avoid the whole genre. If a real story is genuinely the best vehicle, tell it plainly with specifics, no engineered arc and no "here's what it taught me:" hinge.

## Body patterns

- **Binary contrast, the single most overused AI structure.** "It's not about X. It's about Y.", "The problem isn't X. It's Y.", "X isn't the future. Y is.", "Most people think it's X. It's actually Y.", "The bottleneck isn't X. It's Y.", "X is dead. Y is what's next.", "Forget X. Focus on Y.", "The question isn't X. The question is Y." → Use a contrast only when specific evidence carries it, and rarely. Banned otherwise.
- **Fill-in-the-blank thought-leader templates:** "X isn't just Y. It's Z." ("AI isn't just a tool. It's a thought partner."), "X is the new Y." ("Prompt engineering is the new programming."), "If you're still doing X, you're already behind.", "In 2026, X won't be optional. It'll be table stakes.", "The best people don't do X. They do Y.", "Your agent is only as good as your context window." → These are generator output. Swap-the-noun templates. Rewrite around the actual decision, mechanism, or number.
- **False agency** (giving inanimate things human powers to avoid naming who acted): "The data speaks for itself.", "The market has spoken.", "The numbers don't lie.", "This technology wants to…", "AI is coming for your job.", "The industry is waking up to…", "The implications are staggering.", "The possibilities are endless." → Name the actor and the number.
- **Colon reveal** (fake drama from a noun phrase, a colon, then a lowercase reveal): "The detail that makes it work: a separate agent grades it.", "The best part: it learns.", "The real story: nobody read the eval." → Rewrite as a plain sentence. Colons are for lists, labels, and quotes, not suspense.
- **Superficial analysis** (a trailing `-ing` clause that pretends to explain significance): "…, highlighting the team's commitment to X", "…, underscoring the shift toward Y", "…, reflecting a broader trend", "…, showcasing what's possible", "…, signaling a new era." → Replace with the actual consequence. "The launch adds file search, highlighting a commitment to better workflows" becomes "The launch adds file search, so users find old drafts without leaving the editor."
- **Importance puffery** (asserting that something matters instead of showing it): "marks a pivotal moment", "stands as a testament to", "plays a vital role in", "solidifies its position as", "underscores the significance of", "represents a major step forward." → State the fact and let the reader judge. "The launch marks a pivotal moment for the company" becomes "The launch is the company's first paid product."
- **Weasel attribution** (a claim with no owner): "experts agree", "studies show", "industry reports suggest", "many argue", "it's widely regarded as", "research indicates", "sources say." → Name the source with a date, or cut the claim. Never invent a source. If there is no source, ask before publishing. **Treat this as the hardest rule in the file:** the entire positioning rests on claims a reader can go check.
- **Fake-strong verbs** (an inflated verb where a plain one is clearer): "serves as a centralized hub for", "acts as a bridge between", "functions as the backbone of", "represents an opportunity to." → Prefer "is" and "has" when they are clearer. "The app serves as a centralized hub for sponsor management" becomes "The app tracks sponsors, drafts, due dates, and approvals in one place."
- **Synonym cycling** (rotating terms for one thing to avoid repetition): the agent, then the assistant, then the tool, then the system, all meaning a single product. → If the clear word is right, repeat it.
- **Negative listing:** "Not a X. Not a Y. A Z." A cousin of binary contrast with the same tell. → Just say Z.
- **Rhetorical setups:** "What if I told you…", "Think about it:", "Plot twist:", "Enter: X.", and the self-answered question ("Why does this matter? Because…"). → Drop the setup, make the point.
- **Pivot phrases** (fake connective tissue between paragraphs): "But here's where it gets interesting:", "And here's the kicker:", "But that's not even the best part.", "Wait, it gets better.", "Now here's the thing:", "But the real story is:", "And that's just the beginning.", "But wait, there's more." → Use real logical connection or a hard fact to move forward.
- **Qualifier sandwich** (hedging around every claim): "It's worth noting that…", "To be fair…", "That said…", "Don't get me wrong…", "Granted, [obvious counterpoint]…" → Say the claim or drop it.
- **Numbered-list-as-synthesis, artificial scarcity, and the ladder of escalation:** "5 things I learned from X:", "3 mistakes everyone makes:", "I spent 100+ hours so you don't have to.", "I read 50 papers on X. Here's the summary:", "I analyzed 1,000 X. Here's what I found:", plus the stacking of "and not only that…". The real insight count in a "10 things" list is usually 2. Use structure only when the content is genuinely list-shaped.
- **"I asked ChatGPT" and the time-lapse brag:** "I asked ChatGPT to X and the results shocked me.", "I built this in 2 hours.", "From zero to X in 48 hours.", "What used to take 3 months now takes 3 minutes." → Lazy AI-era formats that read as unserious to the target audience.
- **Dramatic fragmentation** (choppy lines as fake style): "Let that sink in.", "Read that again.", "Full stop.", "Period.", "That's it. That's the post.", "This. Is. The. Future.", "Sit with that for a second.", "I'll say it louder for the people in the back." → A real paragraph beats fragments-as-style.

## Closers

- **CTA slop:** "What do you think? Drop your take below.", "Agree or disagree?", "Agree?", "Thoughts?", "What would you add?", "Follow for more X content.", "Repost if this resonated.", "Save this for later.", "Tag someone who needs to hear this.", "Link in comments." → Close on an earned, specific thesis. No CTA.
- **Fake philosophical closer** (profound-sounding, says nothing): "The question isn't whether, but when.", "We're still early.", "This is just the beginning.", "The best X is yet to come.", "The genie is out of the bottle."
- **Performative mic drop:** "And that changes everything.", "Think about that.", "This is the new normal.", "Act accordingly.", "Plan accordingly.", "Welcome to the future.", "Buckle up.", "X will never be the same." → Close on an earned, specific implication instead.
- **Summary-recap ending:** "In conclusion,", "Ultimately,", "Overall,", "To sum up,", "So what does this all mean?", or a final paragraph that restates the piece. The reader was just there. End on the last concrete point, the takeaway, or the real implication.

## Shape tells (the over-corrected slop)

Removing every pattern above and nothing else produces a second, subtler kind of slop: uniformly clipped, evidence-dense, declaratively identical prose. It reads as edited-by-machine rather than written-by-a-person. Scan for shape, not just phrases:

- **Robotic rhythm:** three or more consecutive sentences with the same shape (subject, verb, number). Every paragraph the same length. Every block opening with a claim and closing with a stat. Vary the shape unless a repetition is doing real work.
- **Uniform tidiness:** every paragraph equally polished, no digression, no aside, no change of pace. Real writing is uneven.
- **Stacked punchy fragments:** already banned above as dramatic fragmentation, but it reappears *after* heavy editing, when long sentences get split. Splitting a tangled sentence is good. Splitting a clear one into staccato beats is not.
- **Hedge-stripping:** cutting every "I think", "probably", "my read is" manufactures a confidence the writer does not have. Cut hedges that pad. Keep hedges that carry real uncertainty or match how the writer actually talks.

## Word-level tells

- **Adverbs that scream AI** (3+ in a paragraph = AI wrote it): literally, incredibly, fundamentally, genuinely, essentially, significantly, arguably, undeniably, remarkably, interestingly, notably, particularly, ultimately.
- **Adjectives that mean nothing:** robust, seamless, cutting-edge, groundbreaking, revolutionary, transformative, comprehensive, holistic, game-changing, next-level, world-class.
- **Verbs that sound smart but aren't:** leverage, navigate (challenges), unpack (concepts), double down, spearhead, supercharge, unlock, streamline, reimagine, synergize.
- **Additional near-certain tells:** delve, foster, utilize, facilitate, empower, tapestry, realm, beacon, multifaceted, meticulous, intricate, paramount, elevate, embark, harness, ever-evolving, paradigm shift.
- **Often-empty phrases that delay the point:** "it's important to note", "at its core", "in the age of", "in the world of", "the reality is", "the truth is", "in terms of", "with regard to", "going forward", "in this piece". → Cut when they delay the point. Keep one occasionally if it is genuinely part of the writer's spoken voice and the sentence still earns its place.

## Comment / reply slop

- **Agreement slop:** "This.", "Exactly this.", "So well said.", "This is gold.", "Couldn't agree more.", "Spot on.", "Saving this.", "Couldn't have said it better." → A reply must add a data point, a correction, a precedent, or a real implication, or it doesn't ship.
- **Engagement-farming comment:** "Counterpoint:" + rephrase, "Hot take:" + restate the post, "As someone who [credentials], I can confirm.", and generic questions that add nothing.
- **Humble-brag response:** "As someone who's been doing this for 10 years…", "I literally just had this conversation with my CEO.", "My team was just discussing this."
- Open the reply with the insight, never with flattery.

## What not to touch

The blocklist above is subtractive. This section is the limit on it. Over-application costs more credibility than the original slop, because generic-but-clean reads as machine-edited to exactly the senior audience being targeted.

Before editing any draft, note 3 to 5 voice signals to preserve: vocabulary, cadence, bluntness, humor, real uncertainty, digressions, level of polish. Keep the note internal. Then:

- **Make the minimum effective edit.** Fix the patterns above. Leave strong sentences alone. A rough draft with a real voice should sound like the same person afterward.
- **Keep hedges that carry meaning.** "I think", "maybe", "my read is", "to be honest" stay when they express genuine uncertainty, self-awareness, or spoken rhythm. They go only when they pad.
- **Keep the edge.** Strong opinions, blunt phrasing, humor, self-interruption, and honest admissions of being wrong belong to the writer. Do not replace them with safer or more professional wording.
- **Keep a personal aside that earns its place.** The ban is on the manufactured story arc, the engineered lesson, and the "here's what it taught me" hinge. It is not a ban on referencing something that actually happened, told plainly with specifics.
- **Do not front-load everything.** Lead with the point when the setup adds nothing. Keep a setup that creates real context or tension.
- **Do not reorganize for neatness.** Preserve the argument's progression and its detours unless the structure is actively hurting the piece. If it is reorganized, say why.

## Detect mode

When the ask is "is this AI slop" or "audit this" rather than "publish this", do not rewrite and do not score.

For each pattern found: name the pattern from this file, quote the offending line, give the fix in a few words. Then stop and offer to edit.

Never claim AI wrote something. Detectors guess. Named patterns are evidence the reader can check.

## LinkedIn-only patterns

These do not apply on X and are deliberately kept out of the shared block.

- **Engagement-bait gating (hard block):** "Comment 'GUIDE' and I'll send you the template.", "Want the checklist? Comment 'YES'.", "DM me 'AI' for the playbook.", "Repost ♻️ to help your network.", "Follow me for daily PM posts." → This is the dominant 2026 reach-hack and the fastest way to look like a growth-hacker rather than a leader. Never. If there is a deeper asset, offer it as an open link, no gating.
- **Humble-brag-as-announcement:** "Humbled and honored to announce…", "Grateful doesn't begin to cover it.", "Still pinching myself." → Avoid the genre.
- **Corporate-LinkedIn filler:** "move the needle", "north star", "low-hanging fruit", "circle back", "drive impact", "deliver value", "at the end of the day", "in today's fast-paced world", "let's dive in", "let's unpack this", "this is a masterclass in X", "chef's kiss", flat rule-of-three ("Clear. Concise. Compelling.").

## LinkedIn formatting tells (visual, not just verbal)

- **Broetry, one-sentence-per-line spacing.** Every sentence its own paragraph with blank lines, engineered for the "…see more" cutoff. Catch: 5+ consecutive single-line paragraphs. Use real paragraphs and let the argument flow. Some line breaks for a genuinely standalone line are fine; the *wall of single lines* is the tell.
- **"…see more" cliffhanger hook** deliberately cut mid-thought ("The email said three words…"). The hook should be a complete, specific observation, not a withheld one.
- **Emoji bullets and section headers:** "✅", "🔹", "➡️" lists; "🔑 Key takeaway:", "⚡ The shift:", "📌 Bottom line:" headers; the strategic single hook emoji (🚀💡🔥🧵👀🎯). House rule: **no emojis in the post body.** A carousel may use the restrained accent system in `visual-system.md`, never emoji.
- **Orphan arrows:** "↓", "👇" on their own line pointing at a CTA or "link in comments." Cut.
- **All-caps single-word emphasis lines:** "This.", "STOP.", "Read." Cut.
- **Em-dash overuse.** House rule: no em-dashes at all. Use commas or periods.

## The high-risk tells for a product-leader voice specifically

These read as inauthentic fastest to the exact senior audience Sai is trying to reach, and are precisely what an LLM defaults to when prompted "write a thought-leadership LinkedIn post." Enforce hardest against:

1. The manufactured-mentorship anecdote ("A junior PM asked me…").
2. The fake-metric brag with a tidy framework ("We 10x'd activation in 6 weeks. Here's the 5-step framework.").
3. The "shipping is a mindset" / reframe-template post.
4. Comment-gating CTAs.

## Self-check before publishing

Answer each as pass or fail. **Any fail means fix the draft and run the whole list again.**

**Openers and closers**

1. Could the first two lines sit unchanged under any AI announcement or any generic LinkedIn post? Fail if yes.
2. Any throat-clearer, false-exclusivity, urgency, or fake-vulnerability opener present? Fail if yes.
3. Any CTA, gating ask, fake-philosophical closer, mic drop, or summary recap present? Fail if yes.

**Patterns**

4. Any binary contrast, negative listing, fill-in-the-blank template, or colon reveal? Fail if yes and the contrast is not carried by specific evidence.
5. Any trailing `-ing` significance clause, importance puffery, or fake-strong verb? Fail if yes.
6. Does every claim carry a named source with a date, or is it Sai's own observation? Fail if any claim rests on "experts agree", "studies show", or an unattributed number.
7. Any pivot phrase, rhetorical setup, dramatic fragmentation, or synonym cycling? Fail if yes.

**Substance**

8. Does every paragraph carry a fact, a comparison, a mechanism, or an advance in the argument? Fail if any line only decorates.
9. Do vague adverbs, empty adjectives, or smart-sounding verbs cluster anywhere (3+ in a paragraph)? Fail if yes.
10. Is there at least one sentence only Sai would write, because of a real tradeoff, adoption bottleneck, or operating consequence he has seen? Fail if no. Add it or kill the post.
11. Would Sai say this out loud to another PM in a serious conversation? Fail if it would sound like a performance.

**Shape and voice**

12. Three or more consecutive sentences sharing the same shape, or every paragraph the same length? Fail if yes.
13. Would Sai recognize this as his own voice, with his hedges, edge, and cadence intact? Fail if it reads as cleaned-up-generic.

**House rules**

14. No em-dashes, no emojis in the body, no engagement-bait CTA, no broetry wall of single lines. Any violation is a fail.
